~21 mbd
The world's most critical oil chokepoint — ~21% of global petroleum flows through this 21-mile-wide passage controlled by Iran's IRGC Navy. Since US-Iran hostilities began (Feb 28, 2026), tankers run at maximum speed through the strait. Israel struck the Shahran oil depot near Tehran on Mar 8, and Iran has threatened to close Hormuz entirely. Brent surged from $77 to $99 in a week, spiking to $119 intraday.

~8.2 mbd

Tankers still running the "cannonball run" at 15-16 kts. War risk insurance: 0.5-1.0% of hull value per transit.

+3,500 nm
With both Middle East chokepoints under fire, the Cape route is now the only safe passage for oil tankers. Adds 10-14 sailing days and $200-400/TEU per voyage. ~50% of tankers already rerouted here from Houthi attacks; more are diverting from Hormuz since the war began. Vessel availability is tightening and freight rates are spiking.
Thesis Connection — The US-Iran War & Maritime Disruption
The "Cannonball Run" — How the War Moves Oil Prices Hour by Hour
Two chokepoints, one war: The US-Iran conflict has created a historically unprecedented situation — both the Strait of Hormuz (~21 mbd) and Bab el-Mandeb (~8.2 mbd) are under simultaneous threat. Iran controls Hormuz directly through the IRGC Navy, and wages a proxy campaign at Bab el-Mandeb through the Houthis. Together, nearly 30% of global oil supply is at risk.

The "cannonball run": Oil tankers transiting these danger zones accelerate to maximum speed (15-16 knots vs. the normal economic speed of ~12 knots) to minimize exposure time. In commodities trading, this is the "cannonball run." Since Operation Epic Fury (Feb 28, 2026), tankers in the Persian Gulf are running the gauntlet past Iranian naval assets while Houthi drones and missiles target vessels in the Red Sea.

Why it moves prices by the hour: Each transit now costs $0.5-2M in war risk insurance alone (for a $100M VLCC — higher for Hormuz, lower for Bab el-Mandeb). Faster transit burns more fuel. Crew demand hazard pay. Some tankers refuse to transit at all. This cascading disruption — insurance, fuel, labor, rerouting, vessel scarcity — compounds across thousands of daily voyages and is priced into Brent crude in real time. Notably, the thesis's H1 regression found that sustained attack frequency actually correlates with decreased volatility over time — a "new normal" effect where markets adapt to persistent disruption. The acute price shocks visible above occur at escalation points (war onset, chokepoint closure), not from cumulative attack counts.

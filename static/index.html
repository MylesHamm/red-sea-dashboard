Thesis Connection: The US-Iran War & Maritime Disruption
The "Cannonball Run": How the War Moves Oil Prices Hour by Hour
Two chokepoints, one war: The US-Iran conflict has placed the Strait of Hormuz (~21 mbd) and Bab el-Mandeb (~7 mbd) under simultaneous threat. The IRGC Navy can disrupt Hormuz from Iranian coastal positions, while Iran-backed Houthis target vessels at Bab el-Mandeb. The Strait of Hormuz remains an international waterway (shipping lanes pass through Omani waters), but IRGC fast-attack boats and anti-ship missiles give Iran credible disruption capability. Combined, over 20% of global seaborne oil faces elevated risk.

The "cannonball run": Oil tankers transiting these danger zones accelerate to maximum speed (15-16 knots vs. the normal economic speed of ~12 knots) to minimize exposure time. In commodities trading, this is the "cannonball run." Since Operation Epic Fury (Feb 28, 2026), tankers in the Persian Gulf are running the gauntlet past Iranian naval assets while Houthi drones and missiles target vessels in the Red Sea.

Why it moves prices by the hour: Each transit now costs $1-2M in war risk insurance alone (1-2% of hull value for a $100M VLCC). Faster transit burns more fuel. Crew demand hazard pay. Some tankers refuse to transit at all. This cascading disruption (insurance, fuel, labor, rerouting, vessel scarcity) compounds across thousands of daily voyages and is priced into Brent crude in real time. Notably, the thesis's H1 regression found that sustained attack frequency actually correlates with decreased volatility over time, a "new normal" effect where markets adapt to persistent disruption. The acute price shocks visible above occur at escalation points (war onset, chokepoint closure), not from cumulative attack counts.

KEY FINDING

MARKET ADAPTATION TO SUSTAINED MARITIME DISRUPTIONS

After controlling for 7 macroeconomic variables using GARCH conditional variance as the dependent variable, none of the three hypotheses are supported in their predicted direction. H1 (β = −0.0031, p = 0.802) and H2 (β = −0.0811, p = 0.215) are not statistically significant. Only H3 chokepoint attacks reach significance (β = 0.1684, p = 0.012), representing the sole variable with marginal predictive power. Oil markets have largely priced in the sustained Red Sea disruption campaign.

CHOKEPOINT VULNERABILITY

Attacks at the Bab el-Mandeb strait represent irreducible geographic risk that markets cannot fully price away or diversify.

MACROECONOMIC DOMINANCE

DXY, OVX, China PMI, and Baker Hughes rig count explain ~46-49% of volatility variance, absorbing the effects of general attack volume.

MARKET ADAPTATION

The significant negative coefficients for H1 and H2 indicate that sustained Houthi operations have become priced into market expectations over the 24-month campaign.

IMPLICATIONS FOR PRACTICE

TRADING DESKS

Use market adaptation metrics not incident counts. Key indicators: % traffic rerouting, insurance premium changes, carrier announcement frequency, trading days since campaign onset.

RISK MANAGEMENT

Incorporate adaptation-state classifier. Shock phase (0–90 days) vs adapted state. GARCH modeling superior to event-count approaches.

GOVERNMENT ANALYSTS

Attack frequency alone provides incomplete threat picture. Integrate logistics data, carrier behavior, market adaptation indicators.

LIMITATIONS

  • Temporal scope: 24-month window may not capture full adaptation cycle
  • Data constraints: ACLED prioritizes lethal incidents, may underreport near-misses
  • Model constraints: GARCH(1,1) cannot capture asymmetric volatility (leverage effect)
  • External validity: Findings may be context-specific to Houthi campaign
  • Uncontrolled confounders: macro shocks, regional tensions occur contemporaneously

FUTURE RESEARCH

  1. Comparative chokepoint analysis (Hormuz, Malacca)
  2. Attack type disaggregation (missiles vs drones vs mines vs seizures)
  3. Freight rate analysis as alternative dependent variable
  4. Machine learning for nonlinear thresholds
  5. Game theory modeling of adversary strategic timing

FINAL ASSESSMENT

The Houthi Red Sea maritime campaign demonstrates that sustained sub-state disruptions affect oil markets through mechanisms fundamentally different from conventional geopolitical shocks. Raw incident counts provide misleading volatility indicators during sustained campaigns. Effective business intelligence requires tracking market adaptation indicators: routing decisions, insurance adjustments, and temporal distance from campaign initiation. The adaptive volatility framework provides empirically grounded alternatives that better match observed market behavior.

Methodology

This dashboard supports a Master's thesis examining the relationship between Houthi maritime attacks and Brent crude oil price volatility. Three hypotheses are tested:

  • H1: Attack frequency correlates with increased Brent price volatility (weekly rolling window OLS regression).
  • H2: Tanker-specific attacks produce larger price movements than general commercial vessel attacks (dummy variable regression).
  • H3: Attacks in the Bab el-Mandeb chokepoint zone produce larger price movements than attacks elsewhere (geographic proximity model).

All regressions use Newey-West HAC standard errors to account for heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation. Control variables include the DXY index and OVX volatility index. The study period spans October 2023 (onset of Houthi attacks) through the present, with the US-Iran war (February 2026) representing a structural break in the time series.
